# Finding the domain expert

The system can already predict and recommend. What it cannot do is know how a
real procurement professional actually decides — and that judgement is the part
worth owning, because it isn't written down anywhere public.

This file holds the outreach messages and the interview script. Send the
messages yourself; automated LinkedIn outreach gets accounts restricted.

**Target:** two people who say yes. Message ten to get there.

---

## 1. Connection request note

A free LinkedIn account caps this at **200 characters**, not the 300 Premium
gets — so write to 200 and it works either way. This one is 189, and it is the
one that actually got accepted.

> Hi [First name] — I built a tool that warns SMEs about supplier delays 48h
> early (du SME Challenge). Your sourcing experience is what I'm missing. Could
> I ask you 20 minutes? Not selling anything.

**Why it works:** names a specific problem, cites something real so it doesn't
read as a template, asks for a small fixed amount of time, and removes the
thing they're bracing for ("not selling anything"). Use their first name only
— leaving the brackets in is the fastest way to look automated.

## 2. First message after they accept

> Thank you for connecting, [First name].
>
> Some context on what I'm building. It watches a small business's stock,
> suppliers and orders, and flags a likely supplier delay about 48 hours
> before it lands — then suggests where to source instead. It's working; I
> built it for the du SME Resilience & Innovation Challenge.
>
> Here's my problem. The software works, but I'm guessing at the part that
> actually matters: how someone in your role decides. When you chase a
> supplier, what you check before switching to another one, how long you wait
> before escalating. I can't get that from a search engine, and getting it
> wrong makes the whole thing useless.
>
> Could I ask you about it for 20–25 minutes? Whenever suits you — a call, or
> written answers if that's easier.
>
> Two things in return: I'll send you a written summary of what I learn, and
> you'll see the tool before anyone else.
>
> Either way, thank you for reading this far.
>
> [Your name]

## 4. The interview

Ask permission to record. Take notes anyway.

Do not pitch. Do not defend the product. You are there to find out where your
assumptions are wrong — every correction is worth more than an agreement.

### Warm-up

1. Could you walk me through what you're responsible for day to day?
2. Roughly how many suppliers do you deal with in a normal week?

### How they see trouble coming

3. **How do you usually find out a supplier is going to be late?** Is there
   something you notice before they tell you?
4. Are there suppliers you already watch more closely than others? What put
   them on that list?
5. Has there been a time you saw it coming and it still went wrong? What
   happened?

### The decision itself — the most important part

6. **When a delivery is at risk, what's the first thing you do?**
7. **What do you check before moving an order to a different supplier?**
   *(Push for the full list — price, lead time, quality, payment terms,
   relationship, minimum order. Ask "what else?" until they run out.)*
8. When do you decide it's not worth switching and you'd rather wait?
9. How long do you wait for a reply before you chase?
10. Who has to approve it, and above what value?

### How they communicate

11. **Could you show me a message you'd actually send a supplier in that
    situation?** *(A real one, with names removed. This is gold — it gives you
    the tone, the length, the wording.)*
12. Email, phone or WhatsApp — which for what?
13. Is the wording different for a supplier you've used for years versus a new
    one?

### The boundaries — what the system must never do

14. **What decision would you never let software make on your behalf?**
15. If a system drafted a message and a purchase order for you, would you want
    to approve them before they went out, or after?
16. **What would make you stop trusting a tool like this?**

### Their world specifically

17. What's true about supply in your market that someone from outside wouldn't
    know? *(Seasonality, Ramadan and Eid demand, customs, local holidays,
    payment culture.)*
18. What do you keep in your head or on paper that isn't in any system?

### The value question

19. **What decision eats hours of your week that you wish something else would
    just handle?**
20. If this worked exactly as you wanted, what would you stop doing?

### Closing

21. Who else should I be talking to?
22. Would you be willing to look over what I write up and tell me where I've
    got it wrong?

## Notes

- **Silence is your friend.** After you ask a question, wait. The useful answer
  usually comes after the pause, not before it.
- **Chase the specifics.** "It depends" is not an answer — ask "depends on
  what?" until you get the actual rule.
- **Write down their exact words.** The phrases they use go straight into the
  system's messages. That's what makes it sound like the trade rather than
  like software.
- **Talk to two or three people.** Where they agree is the rule. Where they
  disagree is where the system needs to ask a human.
- **The last question matters most.** Someone willing to review your write-up
  is someone who might put their name to it — and that signature is what turns
  this from a project into something you own.

## 6. Written variant — when they won't take a call

Some people will answer in writing but never book a call. For them, put the
questions in the message itself. Seven is about the ceiling; past that the
reply rate falls off a cliff, so these are the seven that carry the most.

> Thanks for accepting, [First name].
>
> Some context. I've built a working tool for the du SME Resilience &
> Innovation Challenge. It watches a small business's stock, suppliers and
> orders, flags a likely supplier delay about 48 hours before it hits, and
> suggests where to source instead. The software side works.
>
> What I don't have is the judgement behind it — and that's what decides
> whether it's actually useful or just another dashboard. You've run
> procurement here; I haven't.
>
> I know your time is short, so I've written the questions out instead of
> asking for a call. Short answers are genuinely fine — even one line each
> tells me more than I have now. Answer the ones you feel like and skip the
> rest.
>
> 1. What usually tells you a supplier is going to be late, before they tell
>    you themselves?
> 2. When a delivery is at risk, what's the first thing you actually do?
> 3. What do you check before moving an order to a different supplier? Price,
>    lead time, quality, payment terms, relationship — what else, and in what
>    order?
> 4. How long do you wait for a reply before you chase, and how do you chase —
>    email, phone or WhatsApp?
> 5. Could you paste a message you'd actually send a supplier in that
>    situation, with names removed? This one would help me more than all the
>    others put together.
> 6. What decision would you never let software make on your behalf, even if
>    it got it right most of the time?
> 7. [One question drawn from their own headline — the thing they list that
>    nobody else does.]
>
> Whatever you send back, I'll write up what I learn and share it with you,
> and you'll see the tool before anyone outside the challenge does.
>
> Thanks either way.
>
> [Your name]

**Question 5 is the one that matters.** A real message gives you the trade's
own wording, and that wording goes straight into what the system sends. It is
worth more than the other six combined.

**Question 7 is where the vertical might change.** Ask about the speciality
they put in their own headline. If the answer runs long and warm, the valuable
problem may not be the one you started building.

**If the reply is two words**, don't take it as failure. Pick the single most
promising line and go deeper on it — "you mentioned X, could you say a bit more
about that?" One answered question beats seven ignored ones.
